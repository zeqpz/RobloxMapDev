Southwest City Reference — Albuquerque & El Paso

Street-level, satellite and 3D reference for a Houston-inspired Southwest roleplay city in Roblox.

791 images · 436 Street View · 265 3D obliques · 90 satellite · 92 locations · Street View dated 2012–2026

Overview

I walked Google Street View, Maps satellite and Google Earth 3D through hoods, commercial strips and downtowns in Albuquerque and El Paso. Every image is filed under shots/<city>/<category>/, and every image in this page links back to the exact spot in Google Maps so you can keep walking around in the real thing.

Each location has up to three kinds of view:

  • Street View: eye-level photos. For a single building I shot it from the nearest panoramas on every street around it, each one aimed at the building, so you get the front, sides and back where a road reaches them. For a street I stepped down the block and shot both sides at each stop.
  • 3D obliques: Google Earth's photoreal 3D, orbited from the four corners (NE, SE, SW, NW) about 55–60° down. These show roofs, parapets, rooftop units and the back of a lot, which Street View can't reach. House close-ups orbit a single house picked from the street walks.
  • Satellite: a straight-down plan view with street names, for footprints, lot layout, parking and road geometry.
What matters most for the build
  • Both cities are low and wide. Most of the city is one-story buildings on big lots, set behind walls and fences, along 6-lane arterials. The skyline is just one cluster of towers downtown. A believable map is mostly ground-floor detail (walls, gates, signs, poles, wires), not tall buildings.
  • Albuquerque reads as stucco: flat-roof boxes with parapets in sand, tan, salmon and white, plus coyote fences (rough wooden sticks), CMU block walls, gravel yards, bare cottonwoods and the Sandia Mountains behind everything.
  • El Paso reads as brick and rock: buff and red brick, river-rock walls everywhere (yards, arterials, retaining walls), painted block shops with hand-lettered signs, palms mixed with mesquite, and the Franklin Mountains splitting the city.
  • Security hardware is the hood signature in both cities: iron bars on windows and doors, iron fences between stucco or rock pillars, chain-link, roll-up shutters on shops.
  • Overhead power lines on wooden poles run along almost every hood street and alley. Cheap to build, and they add a lot of atmosphere.
  • Don't copy real brands into the game. Keep the shapes and colours (canopy gas stations, striped A-frame burger stands, tan big-box stores) and use made-up names and logos.

Albuquerque findings

Hoods: International District ("War Zone"), south of Central between San Mateo and Wyoming

  • A tight street grid of 1950s–60s one-story houses: flat-roof stucco boxes and low-pitch ranch houses with shingle roofs. Lots are about 15 m wide.
  • Two-story fourplexes in brick or stucco, often with a diamond or arch motif on the stair tower, behind gravel parking lots. These are the signature War Zone building.
  • Front-yard edges are a mix of white or black iron fences on stucco pillars, CMU block walls (sometimes half stuccoed), chain-link, and dirt or gravel yards. Bins sit at the curb.
  • Streets have rolled concrete curbs, narrow attached sidewalks, wooden utility poles with transformers, and bare elms and cottonwoods.

Old barrios: Barelas, Martineztown, Wells Park

  • Older adobe and pueblo-style homes: flat roofs with parapets and roof drains (canales), some pitched metal roofs, brick infill and small porches. Massing is softer and more irregular than in the War Zone.
  • Coyote fences (vertical wooden latillas), chain-link, iron gates and big shade trees. Murals on community buildings (Wells Park).
  • Martineztown has old stucco commercial blocks with stepped "mission" parapets, a good corner-store template.

NE Heights, South Valley, Westside

  • NE Heights: 1960s ranch homes with low-pitch shingle roofs, attached garages, gravel yards and wide streets with bike lanes. The Sandias fill the end of every east-facing street.
  • South Valley (Isleta Blvd / Arenal): semi-rural. Big lots, mobile homes, dirt shoulders, wide open parking lots and low commercial boxes.
  • Westside: newer tan stucco subdivisions behind walls along the arterials, and red-painted fire-lane curbs.

Route 66 / Central Ave strips

  • A 6-lane arterial with center-running bus lanes (red-tinted pavement, island stations), raised medians, galvanized mast-arm signals and green street-name blades.
  • One-story stucco strip buildings with parapets and pueblo towers, car washes, fast-food pads, motels and pole signs. Lots of empty lots and chain-link between them.
  • Nob Hill is the "nice" strip: Pueblo-Deco and Streamline storefronts, new 3-story mixed-use buildings, decorative lamp posts and landscaped medians.

Gas stations, stores, food

  • Gas stations use flat canopies with a coloured fascia band over 2–6 pump islands, a separate convenience store box (stucco or split-face block), raised landscape islands with gravel and boulders, and a tall price pylon at the corner.
  • The ethnic grocery (Talin) is a good "Southwestern box" template: coloured stucco blocks (orange, green, lavender) stepped in height, with an iron perimeter fence.
  • Big-box stores have plain tan parapet walls, an entry vestibule and a loading dock at the back.

Downtown and offices

  • 1920s brick and terra-cotta blocks at 4th & Central, the KiMo (Pueblo Deco), ART bus stations, and 1990 towers (Albuquerque Plaza: 22 floors, pinkish stone, pyramid roofs).
  • Uptown has 1980s office blocks around big parking lots and decks.

El Paso findings

Segundo Barrio and Chihuahuita (by the border)

  • Two-story red-brick tenements with corbelled brick cornices, flat roofs and iron balconies or door gates. One-story brick rows open straight onto the sidewalk.
  • Dirt and gravel alleys about 4–5 m wide behind the blocks, with sheds, chain-link and wires overhead.
  • Painted block in pastel blue, pink and turquoise, iron security grilles on every opening, and murals. Newer affordable housing is 3–4 story tan stucco with coloured accents.

Central and East Side neighborhoods

  • Manhattan Heights / Five Points: 1920s brick bungalows, tan-brick 1–2 story commercial on Montana Ave, murals and painted ads.
  • East Side: 1970s–90s brick-front ranch houses. Arterials are lined with continuous rock walls (the backs of subdivisions), with xeriscape strips and white iron fences in front.

Commercial strips: Alameda, Dyer, Montana, S El Paso St

  • Alameda Ave (Lower Valley) is the most "hood" strip: painted block shops (orange, pink), party-supply and tire shops, used-car lots, taquerias and hand-painted signs.
  • S El Paso St is a dense downtown shopping street by the bridge: 2–3 story historic fronts, steel roll-up shutters, "zapatería" signs, cast-iron lamp posts and string lights.
  • Strip malls have arcades (columns or arches) over the walkway and tan stucco with a sign band. Rock retaining walls with buttresses run along the parking-lot edges.

Gas stations, food, stores

  • The same canopy-and-box layout as Albuquerque, but with more palms, river-rock planter walls and brick store fronts. Truck-stop sized stations on the far East Side.
  • Food: striped A-frame burger stands, 1950s flat-roof taco diners (Chico's Tacos), and fast-food pads with striped facades.

Downtown and civic

  • A compact tower cluster: Plaza Hotel (Pueblo Deco), Wells Fargo Plaza (dark glass and stone), the new blue-glass WestStar Tower, and the red-brick Union Depot with its clock tower.

  • Signals: galvanized steel mast arms over the lanes. Black heads in ABQ, yellow heads with backplates in EP (Texas). Green street-name blades on the arm, push-button posts at the corners.
  • Lighting: cobra-head lights on davit poles along arterials, or bolted to wooden utility poles in the hoods. Decorative lamp posts in Nob Hill, downtown and S El Paso St.
  • Markings: continental (zebra) crosswalks, white stop bars, turn arrows, bike lanes. Paint is sun-faded, asphalt is cracked and patched, and some intersections are concrete.
  • Drainage: concrete-lined channels and arroyos in ABQ (North Diversion Channel) with chain-link and a maintenance track. El Paso has the concrete-lined Rio Grande at the border with fencing.

Terrain & plants

  • Albuquerque (≈1,500–1,800 m elevation): the city tilts up eastward ("the Heights") to the Sandia Mountains, a sheer granite wall with a pink cast at sunset. The flat West Mesa with its volcanic escarpment is to the west. The Rio Grande runs through the middle in a green band of cottonwood forest (the bosque). Dry arroyos cut the slopes.
  • El Paso (≈1,120–1,250 m): the Franklin Mountains (brown and red rock, little vegetation) run north–south through the city. The Rio Grande is a concrete channel along the border, with Juárez right across it. To the east is flat desert valley floor with tan caliche dirt lots.
  • Plants: cottonwood and Siberian elm (bare grey in winter), juniper, chamisa, yucca, cholla, xeriscape gravel (ABQ). Mexican fan palms, mesquite, desert willow, creosote and oleander (EP). Lawns are rare. Gravel and dirt are the default ground.
  • Light: very clear, high-contrast sun, pale blue sky, long hard shadows. Set Roblox Lighting to a warm, high sun with low haze, and add dust-coloured atmosphere for the desert edge.
  • Roblox terrain: Sand/Ground for lots and desert, Sandstone/Rock/Limestone for the mountains, Grass only for parks, golf courses and the bosque. Put roads and sidewalks on as meshes or parts, not terrain.

What to build first

  1. Road kit: arterial with median, collector, residential street, alley, 4-way intersection with mast arms, crosswalks and curb ramps, driveway cuts.
  2. Prop kit: wooden power pole + wires, cobra-head light, signal mast, bus shelter, swamp cooler, wall AC, dumpster, bins, ice box, propane cage, newspaper box, fire hydrant, palm, cottonwood, juniper, yucca, boulders.
  3. Fence and wall kit: CMU wall, stucco pillar with iron infill, river-rock wall, coyote fence, chain-link (+ barbed top), rolling iron gate.
  4. Houses: 3 flat-roof stucco variants, 1 ranch house, 1 fourplex, 1 EP brick tenement, 1 mobile home.
  5. Commercial: gas station (canopy + store), strip mall (modular bays), corner store/taqueria, Route 66 motel, tire shop, pawn shop.
  6. Anchors: grocery/big box, police substation, school, one hero office tower for the skyline.

Rough triangle budgets for Roblox: props 200–1,500; fence modules 100–600; houses 3–6k; gas station 8–12k; strip mall 10–15k (split into bays). Keep each MeshPart under Roblox's 20k-triangle limit and use 1024² PBR textures (SurfaceAppearance).

How these were captured

  • tools/capture.py drives Chrome headlessly. It geocodes each location with a Maps search, then saves Street View (software GL), Maps satellite and Google Earth 3D (GPU) frames with the map UI hidden or cropped out. All metadata, including each shot's Google Maps link and Street View capture date, is in data/shots.jsonl.
  • To add places, edit tools/subjects.py and run geocode, then street / sat / earth / houses3d <abq|ep>. Existing images are skipped. Then run python tools/build_report.py.
  • Limits: for some businesses the Maps pin is a few tens of metres off, so a couple of shots show the neighbour instead (the 3D orbits cover those lots). About 85% of the Street View shots are from 2022–2026; a few spots only have older panoramas (back to 2012), and each caption shows its date. Earth 3D gets soft up close, so read fine detail from Street View.
